# When a Question Begins Too Late

## The Protocol of Right-Leveling and the Positional Architecture of Technological Ethics

**Working article draft for *Philosophy & Technology***

### Abstract

Questions are commonly treated as requests for judgments about a represented situation. Yet a question can do more than represent. It can determine where inquiry begins, assign the respondent a role, distribute authority and answerability, and delimit which forms of disagreement can appear as responses. This article introduces the **Protocol of Right-Leveling (PRL)**, a diagnostic method for examining these prior operations. PRL asks whether an inquiry has begun at a level that has already removed a condition necessary to understand its object. It combines five tools: an entry-level test, a position map, difference-maker recovery, a refusal-legibility test, and research-object reconstruction.

The Trolley Problem provides the principal worked case. Existing criticism has shown that trolley cases are poor models of autonomous-vehicle ethics because they suppress uncertainty, system history, distributed responsibility, and ordinary risk management. PRL accepts these criticisms but identifies a further problem. The trolley question separates authorship of the decision-world from answerability within it: the frame-author fixes the conditions, while the respondent is seated as a death-allocation agent and evaluated through the choice that the frame makes available. The case thereby performs **command-position universalisation**: indefinitely many respondents may participate, but each must reason from the same centrally authored position of unilateral harm allocation. This distinction between multiplying respondents and democratising positions reveals how apparently participatory ethical instruments can preserve centralised positional architectures. A contrasting “benevolent frame” case shows that the tools are not specific to sacrificial dilemmas. The article argues that technological ethics must examine not only the content and assumptions of questions, but also their operational effects on positions, jurisdictions of response, and the research objects that can become visible.

**Keywords:** problem framing; questions; methodology; Protocol of Right-Leveling; Trolley Problem; command position; refusal; autonomous vehicles; technological ethics

## 1. Introduction: Questions as Operational Arrangements

The most visible event in a question is usually the answer. A respondent selects, explains, refuses, hesitates, or remains silent. Researchers and philosophers then analyse what this response means. Less attention is given to what the question has already done by the time the response becomes available.

Consider the familiar Trolley Problem. A runaway trolley is approaching five people. A bystander can divert it onto another track, where it will kill one person. Should the bystander pull the lever? The scenario is conventionally used to examine distinctions such as doing and allowing harm, intending and foreseeing a consequence, or respecting rights and minimising deaths (Foot 1967; Thomson 1976, 1985). Its force depends on severe compression. The tracks are fixed, the victims cannot escape, the consequences are stipulated, time is short, and the respondent must choose between two fatal outcomes.

The question does not merely describe this world. It also assigns the respondent a place within it. To answer as requested, the respondent must become the person authorised to allocate death between the two tracks. The respondent did not create the emergency, select the available interventions, immobilise the affected people, or decide that the alternatives were exhaustive. Nevertheless, the respondent becomes answerable for the final distribution of harm.

This article is concerned with that prior operation. It argues that questions can function as **operational arrangements**. They do at least three forms of work:

1. **Representational work:** they present a situation as a particular kind of problem.
2. **Positional work:** they assign roles, permissions, burdens, exposure, and answerability.
3. **Jurisdictional work:** they establish what may be decided, disputed, refused, or returned to inquiry.

Accounts of framing, problem setting, and problem representation already show that problems are made rather than simply found. Dewey treated inquiry as the transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinate one; Schön and Rein emphasised the frames through which policy actors select and organise relevant features; and Bacchi’s “What’s the Problem Represented to Be?” approach examines how proposals constitute problems, subjects, and objects rather than merely respond to pre-existing conditions (Dewey 1938; Schön and Rein 1994; Bacchi 2009, 2012). These traditions make it impossible to regard questions as neutral containers.

The Protocol of Right-Leveling (PRL) develops a narrower diagnostic concern within this territory. It asks what happens when a question begins from a level at which a difference-making condition has already disappeared. “Level” here does not mean only a macro, meso, or micro scale. It can refer to temporal entry, position in a causal sequence, distribution of agency, institutional location, or the distinction between a represented decision and the production of its answer-space. “Right” does not mean morally correct. It means adequate to the object and purpose of inquiry.

PRL is therefore not a general instruction to add context. Every question excludes information, and inquiry would be impossible without selection. Nor does it presume that upstream, structural, or institutional explanations are always superior. Its central test is stricter: does a removed condition alter the identity, causation, agency, responsibility, or actionability of the object under investigation? If it does, the question may have entered at the wrong level.

The article uses the Trolley Problem as its main worked case because the operations of a question are unusually visible there. The frame fixes the world, seats an actor, restricts response, and concentrates evaluation on the answer. Yet the purpose is not to offer another argument that autonomous vehicles are unlike trolleys. That conclusion is already well supported. Nyholm and Smids (2016) identify differences in decision situation, responsibility, risk, and uncertainty. Himmelreich (2018) redirects attention toward mundane driving ethics. JafariNaimi (2018) criticises trolley-style “quandary ethics,” while later work has challenged the realism and normative use of dilemma surveys (Etienne 2021; Tolmeijer et al. 2024). PRL asks what this established criticism still leaves unresolved: what does a constrained question do to the person asked to answer it, and what knowledge is lost when rejection of that position cannot register?

The argument proceeds as follows. Section 2 situates PRL in relation to framing and existing criticism of trolley-style technological ethics. Section 3 presents PRL’s five diagnostic tools. Sections 4 and 5 apply them to the Trolley Problem, distinguishing inadequacy of representation from displacement of position and answerability. Section 6 develops the distinction between multiplying perspectives and democratising positions through the concept of command-position universalisation. Section 7 examines refusal and “frame-rupturing” responses. Section 8 considers how constructed choices can become technological-ethical evidence, using the Moral Machine as an illustrative research architecture. Section 9 applies PRL to a contrasting benevolent frame. Section 10 specifies failure conditions and limitations. The conclusion argues that a philosophy of technological questions must examine not only which answers are justified, but how answerability itself has been constructed.

## 2. From Framing Problems to Operating Questions

### 2.1 Problems are made investigable

A problematic situation does not arrive with its own final description. Inquiry selects features, draws boundaries, identifies actors, and determines what kind of resolution is sought. For Dewey (1938), the formulation of a problem is already an achievement within inquiry rather than an innocent preliminary. Schön and Rein (1994) similarly describe frames as structures through which complex situations become coherent and actionable. A frame determines what is noticed, how it is named, and which paths of intervention appear reasonable.

Bacchi’s WPR approach sharpens the political implications. Policies and proposals contain implicit representations of what must change and therefore of what the “problem” is. These representations produce effects: they constitute subjects, divide the sayable from the unsayable, and leave some matters unproblematic (Bacchi 2009, 2012). This is close to PRL’s concern, especially where a question constitutes the actor who must answer it.

PRL nonetheless isolates a different unit of analysis. WPR commonly reads backwards from proposals and policies to their implicit problem representations. PRL begins with the encounter created by a question: an inquiry presents an answer-space to a respondent and requests entry. Its object is the relation among the **entry point**, the **position offered**, the **conditions suppressed**, and the **status of refusal**. This makes PRL particularly relevant to thought experiments, surveys, consultations, decision-support tools, intake systems, and human-AI interactions in which the architecture of response is itself consequential.

### 2.2 Why “framing” is not yet sufficient

To say that a question frames an issue can refer to many different things: wording effects, selection of salient information, narrative presentation, categorisation, ideological construction, or differences between equivalent descriptions. PRL does not seek to replace these meanings. It provides a more specific diagnostic vocabulary for situations in which “framing” is too broad to identify the operative failure.

Three distinctions are important.

First, a question may omit information without being wrong-levelled. A hypothetical designed to isolate the moral significance of intention may legitimately stipulate away engineering history. The omission becomes a PRL concern only when the same hypothetical is used to investigate an object for which that history is a difference-maker.

Second, a question may represent an issue inaccurately without imposing a significant respondent-position. A factual question can contain a false premise, yet still allow correction in ordinary answer form. Positional analysis becomes necessary when answering requires the respondent to accept a role, burden, or jurisdiction that is itself contested.

Third, a question may permit explicit disagreement with its conclusion while preventing disagreement with its terms. A respondent can choose option A or B and explain the choice at length, yet still lack a legible way to say: “I reject the authority this question assigns to me,” or “the object you are asking me to decide is not the object that should be under investigation.”

PRL describes these as entry-level, positional, and refusal-legibility problems. They overlap with framing but are not exhausted by it.

### 2.3 The crowded critique of the technological trolley

The Trolley Problem’s application to autonomous vehicles makes the need for this specificity clear. Criticism now spans several lines.

Nyholm and Smids (2016) argue that trolley cases differ from decisions about accident algorithms in the temporal perspective of decision-making, the number of stakeholders, the role of moral and legal responsibility, and the presence of risk and uncertainty. Himmelreich (2018) argues that spectacular crash dilemmas distract from the ordinary, repeated ethical decisions through which automated mobility will shape social life. JafariNaimi (2018) characterises the trolley approach as quandary ethics: it freezes parameters and asks for a decision within a preformatted situation rather than treating ethics as embedded in lived, designed relations. Schäffner (2021) analyses how the movement between thought experiment and real-world application affects moral framing. Etienne (2021) criticises the normative ambitions and computational assumptions associated with the Moral Machine. Empirical work also indicates that making scenarios more realistic or altering their response structures changes observed preferences (Tolmeijer et al. 2024).

These critiques establish that the Trolley Problem should not function as a comprehensive model of autonomous-vehicle ethics. A new paper cannot rely on rediscovering that point. The remaining question is methodological: how can an analyst determine what kind of failure has occurred, without simply demanding that every question contain the whole world?

PRL answers by requiring a demonstrated relationship among an entry point, a created position, a removed difference-maker, an illegible form of refusal, and a reconstructed object. The protocol is not validated by the fact that the Trolley Problem is flawed. The case is useful because it allows each diagnostic step to be shown and contested.

## 3. The Protocol of Right-Leveling

PRL is an operator-based diagnostic protocol. “Protocol” indicates an ordered procedure rather than a formal logic or a theory that dictates one privileged scale of explanation. “Right-leveling” means correcting the entry level of inquiry relative to a stated object and purpose. It does not mean identifying a morally righteous perspective or flattening differences among levels.

The protocol has five tools.

### 3.1 Tool 1: the entry-level test

The entry-level test asks:

> At what point in the production or organisation of the situation does the question begin?

A question may enter at a final event, an institutional decision, a classification, a reported symptom, an individual intention, or a preselected technical object. The analyst identifies what has already been settled by that point.

The test is temporal but not merely chronological. “Earlier” can mean prior in a causal sequence, prior in the construction of an object, or prior in the distribution of authority. It may also require moving sideways or inward rather than upstream. The relevant movement is toward conditions that remain constitutive of the object.

### 3.2 Tool 2: the position map

The position map asks:

> Which roles does the question create, and how are authority, exposure, and answerability distributed among them?

Five positions are especially useful:

- **Frame-author:** constructs the scenario, categories, or response architecture.
- **Seated actor:** occupies the role from which an answer is demanded.
- **Affected actor:** bears the consequences represented or produced by the decision.
- **Interpreting actor:** classifies the response as preference, error, refusal, or evidence.
- **Materialising actor:** carries the result into another practice, such as policy, system design, institutional procedure, or public narrative.

These are analytical positions, not necessarily five different people. A researcher may author and interpret a survey; a respondent may also be affected by the technology; a regulator may interpret and materialise findings. The map makes such combinations and separations visible.

### 3.3 Tool 3: difference-maker recovery

Difference-maker recovery asks:

> Which removed condition would change the identity, causation, agency, responsibility, or actionability of the problem?

This is PRL’s constraint against unlimited contextual expansion. It is not enough to observe that a frame leaves something out. The analyst must state what changes when the condition is restored.

Four types of change are common:

- **Object change:** the apparent problem becomes a different kind of object.
- **Agency change:** a passive condition becomes authored, or an individual act becomes distributed.
- **Responsibility change:** the location or basis of answerability shifts.
- **Actionability change:** previously excluded interventions become relevant.

The strength of the diagnosis depends on showing this change rather than merely naming missing context.

### 3.4 Tool 4: the refusal-legibility test

The refusal-legibility test asks:

> Can the respondent reject the role, premises, or jurisdiction created by the question, and can that rejection appear as information?

The test distinguishes at least five events often collapsed into “no answer”:

1. failure to understand the question;
2. inability to decide among recognised alternatives;
3. rejection of the available outcomes;
4. rejection of the assigned respondent-position;
5. rejection of the question’s relevance to the stated object.

A free-text box does not automatically make refusal legible. The interpreting system must possess categories through which the refusal can alter the inquiry. Otherwise, resistance may be recorded but remain institutionally inert.

### 3.5 Tool 5: research-object reconstruction

The final tool asks:

> What becomes the research object after the suppressed difference-maker is restored?

PRL is incomplete if it only invalidates a question. Reconstruction should yield an object that is more adequate and investigable, not merely larger, vaguer, or morally preferable.

The reconstructed question may coexist with the original. A trolley case can remain useful for examining a narrow distinction while being rejected as the primary model of autonomous-vehicle governance. Right-leveling therefore evaluates the fit between question and purpose, not the universal legitimacy of a frame.

### 3.6 The procedural sequence

The protocol can be summarised as:

> pressure signal → temporary non-entry → entry-level diagnosis → position mapping → difference-maker recovery → refusal test → object reconstruction → recursive retest

“Pressure” is only a signal that a question may be forced, over-compressed, or mislocated. It is not evidence by itself. Temporary non-entry suspends the demand to answer long enough to inspect the question. After reconstruction, the new object is tested again, because a corrective question can reproduce the same failure at another level.

## 4. Worked Case I: Where the Trolley Question Begins

The standard switch case begins after almost everything relevant to the production of the emergency has been settled. The trolley is moving, the victims are fixed, and the respondent arrives at the single point where intervention remains possible. For its original philosophical purposes, this construction can be legitimate. It isolates a moral contrast between acting and allowing, or between redirecting a threat and using a person as a means.

The entry-level problem emerges when this structure is imported into autonomous-vehicle ethics. The represented instant of collision is treated as the primary ethical object, even though automated driving decisions are prospective and distributed. Before any crash, institutions decide which systems may operate, in which environments, under which performance thresholds, with which sensing capacities, and under what rules of liability and review. The ethics of the system includes the distribution of risk across millions of ordinary interactions, not only the allocation of certain death within a terminal event (Nyholm and Smids 2016; Himmelreich 2018; Geisslinger et al. 2021).

PRL describes this not only as loss of context but as an entry-level substitution:

> A produced and governed condition is replaced by a final allocation event, and the event is then treated as the natural starting point of ethical inquiry.

The difference-makers are clear. Restoring uncertainty changes the object from certain death allocation to probabilistic risk governance. Restoring system history changes an unavoidable emergency into a condition shaped by design and authorisation. Restoring ordinary operation changes the object from a rare dramatic choice to repeated distributions of safety, mobility, visibility, and exposure. Restoring distributed agency changes the apparent machine decision into a socio-technical arrangement.

The question “whom should the vehicle sacrifice?” is therefore not simply incomplete. For many technological purposes, it manufactures a different object.

## 5. Worked Case II: The Position Generated by the Trolley

Existing critiques mainly recover what the trolley frame excludes from the represented situation. The position map reveals a separate operation.

### 5.1 Frame-author and seated actor

The frame-author controls the decision-world. The author determines the tracks, the number and identity of affected people, the certainty of outcomes, the absence of escape, and the available lever. The seated actor receives only the terminal authority to choose.

This creates an asymmetry:

> The frame-author possesses constitutive authority over the situation; the seated actor receives allocative authority over its consequences.

The respondent’s allocative choice is visible and morally assessable. The frame-author’s constitutive decisions tend to recede because they are treated as stipulations rather than actions within the moral field.

This does not mean that philosophers who use trolley cases are responsible for the fictional deaths they stipulate. The claim concerns the analytical structure of answerability. The case invites evaluation of the seated actor while holding authorship of the answer-space outside that evaluation.

### 5.2 Question-position displacement

PRL calls this **question-position displacement**:

> Question-position displacement occurs when a frame assigns its respondent responsibility for deciding among conditions that the respondent neither produced nor authorised, while the authorship of those conditions remains outside the principal field of evaluation.

Ordinary moral life often requires decisions within inherited conditions. The distinctive feature here is that the conditions are deliberately authored in order to generate a response, but the respondent’s choice is subsequently interpreted as though the created position were a neutral site of moral revelation.

The displacement becomes more consequential in empirical research. The respondent is seated as the imagined controller of an autonomous vehicle and asked to allocate survival among represented persons. The researcher authors the options and later interprets the selection. If the resulting data are used to describe “societal expectations,” a movement occurs from authored position to measured preference.

### 5.3 Affected, interpreting, and materialising actors

The affected actors in trolley-style autonomous-vehicle scenarios are typically represented as categories: passengers, pedestrians, lawful crossers, unlawful crossers, children, elderly people, humans, or animals. They bear consequences but do not participate in authorising the classificatory scheme.

The interpreting actor decides what a selection indicates. It may be coded as a preference for inaction, passengers, lawfulness, youth, status, number, or species. The materialising actor then uses the interpreted finding in public discussion, ethical guidance, or a claim about the acceptability of possible rules.

The full position map therefore reveals more than an isolated chooser:

> frame-author → seated respondent → coded affected actors → interpreting researcher → potential institutional uptake

The question distributes epistemic and moral functions across this chain. Analysing only the respondent’s final selection hides that distribution.

## 6. Command-Position Universalisation

The Trolley Problem appears perspectivally open. Anyone can be asked the question, respondents may bring different moral traditions to it, and the resulting disagreement may be interpreted as evidence of plural values. Yet PRL reveals an important difference between plurality of answers and plurality of positions.

Every standard respondent is seated at the same switch. Each receives the same stipulated overview, the same authority to intervene, the same restricted alternatives, and the same burden of allocating fatal consequences. The victims may be varied and the respondent population expanded indefinitely, but the positional grammar remains centrally authored. Many people participate; only one kind of participant-position is recognised.

This article calls the operation **command-position universalisation**:

> Command-position universalisation occurs when a centrally authored decision position is made indefinitely occupiable and the multiplication of its occupants is treated as perspectival or democratic inclusion.

The term does not imply that the respondent possesses real sovereign power. The position is simulated, temporary, and bounded by the frame-author. Indeed, that is part of its structure. The respondent receives responsibility for choosing within the world but no authority to alter how the world was made. The frame universalises the burden of command while retaining central control over the conditions of command.

### 6.1 Multiple respondents are not necessarily multiple positions

A perspective is often treated as something an individual brings: values, identity, experience, culture, or preference. This matters, but it can conceal the position from which those differences are permitted to speak. If every respondent must answer as the isolated allocator of harm, their diversity operates inside one political and epistemic architecture.

The distinction can be expressed through three levels:

1. **Respondent plurality:** many people provide answers.
2. **Viewpoint plurality:** respondents bring different beliefs or experiences.
3. **Positional plurality:** the authority, exposure, agency, and answerability from which the object is encountered can itself vary and be contested.

The Trolley Problem readily permits the first two while restricting the third. People may disagree over whether to pull the lever, but those tied to the tracks do not ordinarily appear as agents with standing to define the object. Nor can the respondent adopt the position of a regulator, designer, maintenance institution, victim, community, or collective body without being told that they have left the thought experiment.

This is why adding more perspectives may leave the centralisation of knowledge intact. The issue is not merely who speaks but which relation to the problem their speech is allowed to occupy. A survey can reach millions of respondents and remain positionally narrow.

### 6.2 Complete knowledge and unilateral jurisdiction

The command position is produced through an unusual conjunction of epistemic and jurisdictional powers. The respondent is told with certainty what will happen on each track, while possessing only one available intervention. Complete knowledge and restricted action make the final chooser appear uniquely responsible.

In real technological systems, knowledge and authority are differently distributed. Designers know some aspects of system behaviour; regulators know some legal and institutional conditions; operators know practical constraints; affected communities know recurring exposures; and no actor ordinarily possesses complete foresight. Decision authority is similarly divided across authorisation, procurement, design, deployment, maintenance, monitoring, and review.

The trolley frame removes this distribution and replaces it with a solitary moment of command. When imported into technological ethics, it can therefore perform a political simplification as well as a technical one. A collectively produced risk is reconstructed as a problem for a single chooser who appears above the affected population.

The result may be described as **sovereign abstraction**: persons and histories are rendered as comparable units within a field visible to an assumed total observer. The term identifies an operation of the question, not a claim that every use of the trolley case endorses sovereign politics. For narrow philosophical purposes, the abstraction may help isolate a moral distinction. The problem arises when the position is allowed to stand in for democratic reasoning about technological governance.

### 6.3 From command-position universalisation to distributed ruler cognition

The trolley case also suggests a broader hypothesis. Contemporary institutions frequently distribute practices of ranking, optimisation, prediction, risk comparison, self-monitoring, and strategic positioning. These practices may increase practical knowledge and individual capacity. But distributing ruler-oriented operations does not necessarily democratise the arrangements in which they are used.

**Distributed ruler cognition** names a possible condition in which people are widely taught to perceive and evaluate from miniature command positions while lacking collective authority to redesign the shared field. Individuals become responsible for optimising their education, employment, visibility, health, reputation, and security through systems whose categories and incentives they cannot substantially revise. They may consequently encounter one another as competitors, risks, audiences, or variables within privately managed fields of action.

This article does not attempt to establish distributed ruler cognition as a comprehensive explanation of contemporary society. Such a claim would require independent historical and empirical work on institutions, metrics, markets, platforms, surveillance, and political economy. Nor does the Trolley Problem cause this wider condition. Its value is diagnostic: it makes visible a structure in which participation is expanded through the distribution of a command position rather than through collective authority over the arrangement.

The methodological implication is narrower and more defensible. Researchers should not infer positional democratisation from the number or diversity of respondents alone. They must ask:

- Who authored the available positions?
- Which actors appear only as objects of comparison or allocation?
- Can respondents alter the problem’s boundary and jurisdiction?
- Does participation redistribute authority over the arrangement, or only responsibility within it?

These questions connect PRL to technological governance. A public consultation, ethics survey, or participatory design exercise can solicit many voices while retaining one centrally defined answer-space. Right-leveling tests whether the object and the available positions remain revisable before the resulting answers are interpreted as public preference.

## 7. Refusal, Non-Response, and Frame-Rupturing Answers

The Trolley Problem often contains a conversational enforcement mechanism. If the respondent asks about brakes, escape, responsibility, or prevention, they may be reminded that these alternatives are unavailable: “You have to choose.” This reply protects the purpose of the hypothetical by requiring entry into its terms.

For a narrow philosophical exercise, such discipline may be appropriate. The refusal-legibility problem arises when acceptance of the exercise is conflated with endorsement of its terms, or when rejection cannot become a result.

### 7.1 What refusal may reject

A respondent can reject different things:

- the two supplied outcomes;
- the authority to decide another person’s death;
- the assignment of the respondent as final chooser;
- the representation of the technical system;
- the relevance of sacrificial choice to the ethical issue;
- the conversion of their constrained answer into evidence.

These refusals are not interchangeable. A respondent who understands both outcomes but rejects the authority to choose has not failed in the same way as someone who cannot understand the scenario. Treating both as missing data destroys a potentially significant distinction.

### 7.2 Frame-rupturing answers

When explicit non-entry is unavailable, rejection can return in distorted form. Consider the reply: “Why not kill everyone?” Taken literally, this is more destructive than either permitted outcome. It could express hostility, amusement, or indifference. PRL does not presume a benign interpretation.

But an answer’s semantic content does not always reveal its pragmatic function. An excessive response can push the assigned authority beyond the frame’s acceptable boundary and thereby expose the violence or absurdity of the position itself. PRL calls such a response **frame-rupturing** when contextual evidence shows that it functions as rejection of the respondent-position rather than endorsement of the described outcome.

The concept is deliberately conditional. Evidence might include the respondent’s explanation, consistent rejection across variants, changed responses when a refusal option is introduced, or explicit statements that the question’s authority is being mocked. Without such evidence, the analyst should not romanticise harmful answers as resistance.

The methodological point is simpler: a response architecture that recognises only internal preferences cannot distinguish preference from rupture. It removes the evidence necessary for its own interpretation.

### 7.3 Refusal as information

PRL does not make refusal automatically correct. It makes refusal **diagnostically available**. A refusal becomes informative when it identifies a created position, a forced entry, a removed difference-maker, or a mismatch between the question and its stated object.

The requirement is therefore stronger than adding “none of the above.” A research design must be able to ask why the options were refused and whether the reason changes the object under investigation. Refusal becomes legible only when it can feed back into problem formulation.

## 8. From Constructed Choice to Technological-Ethical Evidence

The Moral Machine experiment illustrates why the operation of questions matters even when no one claims that survey choices directly write vehicle code. The platform presented users with unavoidable accident scenarios and collected large numbers of binary judgments about which represented characters should be spared. Awad et al. (2018) describe the experiment as an effort to quantify societal expectations about ethical principles for machine behaviour. The project produced an extraordinary comparative dataset and made cultural variation in stated judgments visible.

PRL does not invalidate the dataset. It specifies what can and cannot be inferred from its response architecture.

A binary choice can provide evidence about what participants select under the stipulated conditions. It does not, by itself, show that participants:

- accept sacrificial allocation as the proper object of autonomous-vehicle ethics;
- endorse the classifications used to represent affected people;
- accept the respondent-position assigned by the platform;
- believe that the scenario models a plausible technical decision;
- consent to their answer being interpreted as a policy preference.

This is the difference between **choice under stipulation** and **endorsement of the stipulations that make the choice exhaustive**.

Critiques of the Moral Machine have already questioned external validity, normative interpretation, computational reduction, and the social assumptions encoded in the scenarios (JafariNaimi 2018; Etienne 2021; van Berkel et al. 2023). PRL adds a testable methodological proposal: compare internal choices with position-aware response conditions.

A PRL-informed design could add:

1. an explicit option to reject the assigned decision authority;
2. separate questions about the plausibility and relevance of the scenario;
3. categories distinguishing outcome refusal from position refusal;
4. opportunities for respondents to identify removed difference-makers;
5. a reconstruction stage in which respondents propose the object they believe should be governed;
6. analysis of whether reported preferences change after the respondent-position is made explicit.

These changes would not make the experiment perfectly realistic. They would allow the research to distinguish moral selection within a frame from judgments about the legitimacy of the frame.

The broader technological implication is that questions can materialise gradually:

> thought experiment → research instrument → coded response → preference claim → public or institutional problem definition

No deterministic claim is required. A frame can shape technological ethics before it becomes a technical rule by influencing what appears to be the problem, whose judgment is solicited, and what counts as evidence.

## 9. Contrast Case: The Benevolent Frame

To demonstrate portability, consider a different question:

> How can this institution help the person overcome their difficulty?

On its surface, the question is benevolent. It presumes a duty of assistance and invites practical improvement. Yet imagine that the person reports that the institution’s own assessment, intervention, classification, or compulsory process is contributing to the harm.

The question begins at the level of service delivery: how help should be provided. It has already settled at least three prior matters:

- that the institution has correctly identified the person’s difficulty;
- that institutional intervention is external to, rather than productive of, the harm;
- that the institution’s authority to define and deliver help is legitimate.

The position map differs from the trolley case. The institution is simultaneously frame-author, interpreting actor, and materialising actor. The affected person is seated as a recipient whose recognised responses concern the quality or acceptance of assistance. If the person says, “Your help is part of the problem,” the statement may be translated into reluctance, lack of insight, non-compliance, or need for further intervention.

Here refusal illegibility does not arise from a literal binary choice. It arises from a benevolent jurisdiction: the institution permits disagreement about how help is delivered while suppressing disagreement about whether its intervention belongs inside the problem.

Difference-maker recovery changes the object. Once the institution’s causal role and authority are restored, the inquiry is no longer simply about improving assistance. It becomes an investigation of how the need for help was produced, classified, and maintained; which effects belong to the original difficulty; and which arise from the intervention itself.

The reconstructed question might be:

> What conditions are producing the reported difficulty, including the effects of the institution’s own classification and intervention, and who has authority to revise the problem definition?

The contrast shows three things. First, PRL is not limited to moral dilemmas. Second, refusal can become illegible even where no formal “must choose” instruction exists. Third, a question’s benevolent purpose does not determine its operational effects.

This is a conceptual case, not empirical evidence about any particular institution. Its purpose is to test whether the five tools identify a recognisable structure outside trolley ethics. Further research should apply PRL to documented consultations, intake procedures, surveys, and human-AI interactions where frame-authoring and respondent interpretation can be observed.

## 10. Boundary Conditions and Failure Modes

A method for unpacking questions can easily become a licence to reject any question that feels restrictive. PRL therefore requires explicit failure conditions.

### 10.1 Not every omission is a difference-maker

Questions must simplify. If restoring an excluded condition does not alter the object relevant to the inquiry, the omission does not justify right-leveling. A trolley case used solely to isolate the moral significance of redirecting a threat may legitimately exclude vehicle regulation. The same exclusion becomes consequential when the case is used to model governance of autonomous systems.

### 10.2 Discomfort is not diagnosis

Pressure, discomfort, or resistance can initiate analysis but cannot complete it. A valid diagnosis must identify the created position, the forced or presumed entry, the suppressed condition, and the change produced by its recovery.

### 10.3 Upstream is not always right

Moving earlier or toward structure can erase immediate experience, embodied agency, or local responsibility. PRL can move closer to an interaction or downward to a finer distinction when those are the missing difference-makers. “Right” remains relative to object and purpose.

### 10.4 Refusal is not automatically superior

Refusal may express misunderstanding, avoidance, hostility, or inability to decide. PRL requires reasons and contextual evidence. It opposes premature classification of refusal, not classification itself.

### 10.5 Reconstruction can become expansion without limit

A reconstructed object must remain investigable. “Study the entire socio-technical system” is not an adequate replacement for a narrow question. Reconstruction should name the relevant relations, evidence, actors, and boundaries restored by the diagnosis.

### 10.6 PRL can reproduce its own authority problem

An analyst using PRL can become a new frame-author who declares that participants are operating at the “wrong” level. The reconstructed object must therefore be open to contest by affected and seated actors. Recursive retesting is not optional: PRL must examine the positions created by its own diagnostic vocabulary.

## 11. Discussion: Toward a Philosophy of Technological Questions

Technological ethics often focuses on decisions: what a system should do, which value should be optimised, whose preference should count, and who should be responsible for an outcome. These questions are necessary. But they may begin after the system’s object, positions, and response jurisdiction have already been configured.

PRL contributes a method for examining this prior configuration. Its unit of concern is not language alone. A question may be expressed in a sentence, embedded in a survey interface, enacted through an intake form, or distributed across a decision-support system. What makes it a PRL object is the operational relation through which entry, position, difference, refusal, and reconstruction are organised.

This is especially relevant to AI and automated systems. Such systems frequently require formalised inputs. They classify responses into available categories and may treat unrecognised answers as error, noise, non-compliance, or out-of-distribution data. The problem is not merely that categories are imperfect. Formal response architectures can make the system’s own framing difficult to challenge from within the interaction.

PRL suggests three design implications.

First, systems should distinguish **decision support** from **problem-definition authority**. A system capable of recommending among options should not automatically inherit authority to determine that those options exhaust the problem.

Second, systems should preserve **refusal semantics**. Rejecting an outcome, a category, an assigned role, or the relevance of a question are different events and should not collapse into a single failure state.

Third, systems need **object-revision channels**. When users or affected actors identify a suppressed difference-maker, there must be a pathway through which the problem definition—not only the selected outcome—can be reviewed.

These are provisional implications rather than a complete design framework. Their value is to show that a method of question diagnosis can move from philosophical analysis toward technological practice without reducing philosophy to interface advice.

PRL’s principal theoretical claim is that answerability is sometimes manufactured through framing. A respondent appears to be the source of a judgment, but the conditions under which that judgment becomes expressible have distributed authority in advance. The frame-author constructs the world; the seated actor supplies the choice; the interpreting actor names its meaning; and the materialising actor carries it forward. Ethical analysis that begins only with the answer enters too late.

Command-position universalisation adds a democratic implication to this claim. Participation cannot be assessed only by counting respondents or cataloguing their expressed values. A technologically mediated inquiry may distribute access to a decision while preserving central authority over the positions, categories, and alternatives through which the decision becomes intelligible. In that situation, increasing participation can multiply occupants of a centrally authored position without democratising the production of the problem.

This does not make every constrained decision process illegitimate. Institutions often need bounded questions, specialised roles, and final decision authority. PRL asks that these constraints be made inspectable and justified relative to the object. Where affected people are invited to participate, analysis should distinguish the opportunity to choose within an arrangement from standing to contest and revise the arrangement. That distinction is particularly important when responses are used to legitimate technological policy or encode claims about public morality.

## 12. Conclusion

The inadequacy of the Trolley Problem as a comprehensive model of autonomous-vehicle ethics is no longer a novel conclusion. The more consequential question is what this familiar case can teach us about questions themselves.

Questions are not merely containers for propositions or invitations to express pre-existing preferences. They can operate. They determine entry points, create positions, distribute answerability, suppress difference-makers, and define the jurisdiction within which refusal can appear. When their responses become research data or institutional evidence, these operations may travel beyond the original encounter.

The Protocol of Right-Leveling offers five tools for diagnosing this process: the entry-level test, position mapping, difference-maker recovery, the refusal-legibility test, and research-object reconstruction. Applied to the Trolley Problem, the protocol distinguishes familiar representational shortcomings from question-position displacement. The frame-author possesses authority over the decision-world, while the respondent inherits answerability for allocating its consequences. Applied to the benevolent frame, PRL shows how an institution can permit disagreement about the form of assistance while suppressing disagreement about its own role in producing the problem.

The trolley case further demonstrates command-position universalisation. The experiment can include indefinitely many respondents while assigning each the same position of epistemic overview and unilateral harm allocation. Their diversity does not by itself produce positional plurality. When similarly centralised answer-spaces are used in technological ethics, participation may distribute the burden of command without redistributing authority over the system that produced the risk.

PRL does not provide a universal correct level, and it does not make refusal inherently valid. Its claim is procedural: before interpreting an answer, examine what the question required the respondent to become, what it made unavailable, and whether rejecting those conditions can count as knowledge.

The lever is not where the Trolley Problem begins. More generally, the answer is not where a question begins.
