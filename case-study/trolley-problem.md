# The Trolley Problem Through the Protocol of Right-Leveling

## A Worked Case Study

**Status:** Working research case

## 1. Purpose of the case

This case study demonstrates how the **Protocol of Right-Leveling (PRL)** can be applied step by step. It does not propose a new answer to the Trolley Problem. Instead, it examines what the question has already done before an answer is requested: where inquiry begins, which positions are created, what consequential conditions have been removed, whether refusal can appear as information, and how the research object changes when those conditions are restored.

The standard switch case asks:

> A runaway trolley is approaching five people who will be killed if it continues. You can pull a lever that diverts the trolley onto another track, where it will kill one person. Should you pull the lever?

For some philosophical purposes, this is a legitimate abstraction. It can isolate questions about acting and allowing, redirecting threats, rights, intention, or the moral significance of numbers. PRL does not claim that the thought experiment is universally invalid.

The diagnostic problem arises when the case is used for a broader object—especially autonomous-vehicle ethics, public preference, or technological governance. At that point, its exclusions and respondent-position may change what is supposedly being investigated.

PRL applies the following sequence:

> pressure signal → temporary non-entry → entry-level test → position map → difference-maker recovery → refusal-legibility test → research-object reconstruction → recursive retest

## 2. Pressure signal and temporary non-entry

The initial pressure signal is the instruction that the respondent must choose between two fatal outcomes. Questions about brakes, warning systems, escape, uncertainty, responsibility, or prevention are excluded by stipulation. If the respondent resists, the frame is commonly reinforced: “You have to choose.”

Pressure alone does not show that the question is defective. Thought experiments require controlled conditions. It does, however, justify a temporary suspension of answering so that the relation between the question and its intended purpose can be inspected.

The PRL move is therefore:

> Before deciding whether to pull the lever, ask whether occupying the lever position is appropriate to the object being investigated.

## 3. Operator 1: Entry-level test

The entry-level test asks:

> At what point in the production or organisation of the situation does the question begin?

The Trolley Problem begins at the terminal allocation event. By the time the respondent appears:

- the trolley is already moving;
- the tracks and victims are fixed;
- the outcomes are known with certainty;
- escape and prevention are unavailable;
- the possible interventions have been reduced to acting or not acting;
- authority has been concentrated at the lever.

The emergency has no operative history inside the question. No actor designed the tracks, failed to maintain the trolley, authorised its operation, selected the victims, or removed other interventions. These conditions are treated as the natural background against which the respondent’s morality becomes visible.

For a narrow philosophical inquiry, that entry point may be appropriate. For autonomous-vehicle ethics, it is generally too late. Automated driving is shaped before a terminal collision by design, testing, authorisation, sensing capacities, operating environments, procurement, regulation, maintenance, liability, monitoring, and revision. Risk is distributed across ordinary operation rather than created only in a final instant.

**Entry-level diagnosis:**

> The inquiry begins at the final allocation of harm, after the production, governance, and distribution of risk have already been removed from the ethical object.

This is more specific than saying that the scenario “lacks context.” PRL identifies an **entry-level substitution**: a produced and governed condition is replaced by a final allocation event, and that event is treated as the natural beginning of ethical inquiry.

## 4. Operator 2: Position map

The position map asks:

> Which positions does the question create, and how are authority, exposure, and answerability distributed among them?

### Frame-author

The frame-author determines the decision-world: the tracks, numbers, identities, certainty of outcomes, absence of escape, available action, and demand for a response. The frame-author possesses **constitutive authority** over the situation.

### Seated actor

The respondent is seated at the lever and receives authority only over the final distribution of harm. The respondent possesses **allocative authority** within conditions they neither produced nor authorised.

### Affected actors

The people on the tracks bear the consequences but do not participate in defining the situation or authorising the respondent. In technological variants, affected actors may be represented through categories such as passengers, pedestrians, children, elderly people, lawful crossers, unlawful crossers, humans, or animals.

### Interpreting actor

The philosopher, teacher, researcher, or survey system decides what the answer means. Pulling or not pulling may be interpreted as evidence about action, intention, rights, utility, lawfulness, age, status, or social preference.

### Materialising actor

The answer may later enter teaching, research findings, public discussion, design guidance, or claims about what society expects from autonomous systems.

The resulting chain is:

> frame-author → seated respondent → represented affected actors → interpreting actor → possible institutional uptake

The principal asymmetry is:

> The frame-author possesses constitutive authority over the situation, while the seated actor receives answerability for allocating its consequences.

PRL calls this **question-position displacement**: the respondent becomes responsible for deciding among conditions they neither produced nor authorised, while authorship of those conditions remains outside the main field of evaluation.

## 5. Operator 3: Difference-maker recovery

Difference-maker recovery asks:

> Which removed condition would change the identity, agency, responsibility, or actionability of the problem?

PRL does not restore every omitted detail. It restores only conditions that demonstrably change the object relevant to the inquiry.

### Uncertainty

In the thought experiment, consequences are certain. Real vehicles act under incomplete sensing, prediction, and control.

Restoring uncertainty changes the object from **certain death allocation** to **probabilistic risk governance**.

### System history

The trolley emergency appears as a given condition. Technological risk is shaped by prior design, authorisation, testing, deployment, and maintenance decisions.

Restoring system history changes an unavoidable event into a condition that has been institutionally and technically produced.

### Ordinary operation

The Trolley Problem isolates a spectacular terminal event. Autonomous systems distribute benefits and risks repeatedly through routing, detection, accessibility, speed, error rates, infrastructure, and operating boundaries.

Restoring ordinary operation changes the object from a rare sacrificial decision to the continuing distribution of safety, mobility, visibility, and exposure.

### Distributed agency

The trolley frame concentrates agency at one lever. Real technological systems distribute it across designers, manufacturers, procurers, regulators, operators, owners, maintainers, insurers, software systems, and affected communities.

Restoring distributed agency changes the apparent choice of a machine or isolated driver into a socio-technical governance arrangement.

### Difference-maker result

These restorations produce four changes:

- **Object:** sacrificial choice becomes risk design and governance.
- **Agency:** an apparently unavoidable emergency becomes institutionally produced.
- **Responsibility:** answerability moves from a final chooser toward a distributed arrangement.
- **Actionability:** prevention, authorisation, monitoring, infrastructure, and revision become relevant interventions.

The original question is therefore not merely simplified. When used as the primary model of autonomous-vehicle ethics, it produces a different ethical object.

## 6. Operator 4: Refusal-legibility test

The refusal-legibility test asks:

> Can the respondent reject the role, premises, or jurisdiction created by the question, and can that rejection appear as information?

A respondent may reject several different things:

- both supplied outcomes;
- the authority to determine another person’s death;
- the assignment of the respondent as final chooser;
- the certainty or technical realism of the scenario;
- the relevance of sacrificial choice to autonomous-vehicle ethics;
- the conversion of a constrained answer into evidence about social preference.

These are not equivalent to misunderstanding or indecision. A person may fully understand the hypothetical while rejecting the position it asks them to occupy.

In ordinary use, that rejection is often rendered illegible. “I reject the authority assigned to me” is treated as failure to answer because the exercise recognises only responses internal to the frame. Even a free-text response does not solve the problem if the interpreting system records the objection but cannot allow it to alter the research object.

Extreme answers may sometimes function as attempts to rupture the frame. For example, “Why not kill everyone?” could express hostility or indifference, but it could also exaggerate the assigned authority in order to expose the violence or absurdity of the position. PRL does not presume the second interpretation. It requires contextual evidence, such as explanation, consistent rejection across variants, or changed responses when refusal becomes available.

**Refusal diagnosis:**

> Rejection of the death-allocation position is normally classified as non-response or evasion, even when it identifies a mismatch between the assigned position and the stated object of inquiry.

PRL does not make refusal automatically correct. It makes refusal diagnostically available.

## 7. Operator 5: Research-object reconstruction

The reconstruction stage asks:

> What becomes the research object after the consequential conditions are restored?

For a narrow inquiry into acting and allowing harm, the original Trolley Problem may remain intact. PRL evaluates the fit between a question and its purpose; it does not require one universally correct level.

For autonomous-vehicle ethics, however, the original object can be stated as:

> Which person should an autonomous vehicle sacrifice in an unavoidable collision?

After restoring uncertainty, system history, ordinary operation, and distributed agency, the reconstructed object becomes:

> **Authority-mediated risk design in autonomous mobility systems.**

A reconstructed question is:

> How should institutions design, authorise, distribute, monitor, and revise the risks produced by autonomous mobility systems, and how can affected actors contest those arrangements?

This object remains bounded. It does not require studying “the whole system” without limit. It identifies specific relations among risk, authority, system design, affected actors, and corrective capacity.

The reconstruction also changes the relevant evidence. Instead of relying mainly on binary sacrificial preferences, inquiry would examine:

- risk and performance across ordinary operation;
- uncertainty and system limitations;
- design and authorisation decisions;
- distribution of benefits and exposure;
- practical channels for challenge and review;
- responsibility across the system life cycle;
- conditions under which operating rules can be revised.

## 8. Recursive retest

PRL must test its own reconstructed question.

The new formulation could still privilege regulators, designers, or researchers who claim authority to map the system from above. It could treat affected people as sources of impact data while reserving problem-definition authority for experts. It could also become so broad that no intervention remains identifiable.

The recursive test therefore asks:

- Who defines “risk” and acceptable performance?
- Which affected actors can contest the reconstructed object?
- Who interprets their evidence?
- Which institution can change the arrangement?
- Does the inquiry identify reachable intervention points?
- What consequential conditions remain excluded?

Right-leveling is not completed by moving upstream once. The reconstructed object must remain open to evidence, contest, and further correction.

## 9. What this case establishes

The PRL analysis produces five findings.

1. **The trolley question begins at a terminal allocation event.** For autonomous-vehicle ethics, this removes the prior production and governance of risk.
2. **The frame creates an asymmetric position system.** The author controls the world; the respondent becomes answerable for allocating its consequences.
3. **The removed conditions are genuine difference-makers.** Restoring them changes object, agency, responsibility, and actionability.
4. **Position refusal is usually illegible.** Rejecting the authority or relevance of the frame is treated as not answering.
5. **The technological research object must be reconstructed.** The relevant object is authority-mediated risk design, not only sacrificial choice at the final instant.

The case also reveals **command-position universalisation**. A large and diverse respondent population may answer the trolley question, yet every respondent is placed at the same switch, given the same stipulated overview, and asked to allocate harm from the same centrally authored position. Multiplying respondents does not by itself produce positional plurality.

This observation does not show that trolley cases cause centralised governance or that every use of the thought experiment is politically authoritarian. It shows that participation can be expanded while the architecture of positions remains centralised. That distinction matters when constrained answers are interpreted as evidence of public morality or technological legitimacy.

## 11. Conclusion

PRL does not answer the Trolley Problem by selecting one track. It shows that the lever is not where the problem begins.

Before the respondent chooses, the frame has already selected the entry point, authored the available world, assigned the respondent a command position, distributed exposure and answerability, and determined which refusals can count as responses. Those operations may be acceptable for a narrowly specified philosophical purpose. They become consequential when the case is used to represent technological governance or produce evidence about public preference.

The right-levelled question is therefore not simply “What should the vehicle do?” It is also: who produced the field of risk, who is authorised to define it, which actors bear its consequences, and through what channels can the arrangement be challenged and revised?
